# History of the Atbash cipher

## Writing the name backwards

The name says exactly what the cipher does. Take the Hebrew letters
Aleph, Taw, Bet, Shin — the first, last, second and second-to-last
letters of the Hebrew alphabet — and you get אתבש: *Atbash*. The
name is the key legend of the cipher itself, read off its own
mapping.

## Atbash in the Bible

The oldest known uses hide inside the Hebrew Bible. The Book of
Jeremiah — written around the fall of Jerusalem in 586 BC — calls
Babylon *Sheshach* and the Chaldeans *Lev-kamai*; medieval
commentators recognised that both are ordinary Hebrew words run
through Atbash (בבל *bbl* → ששך *ššk*, כשדים *kśdym* → לבקמי
*lbqmy*). Commentators such as Rashi (1040–1105) and Radak discussed
these passages, which makes Atbash the oldest attested substitution
cipher by a wide margin.

## Why scribes used it

Scholars still argue about intent: a disguise for sensitive names, a
literary game, or a scribal puzzle — the Bible's scribes left no
memo. What is clear is that the alphabet-mirror was part of Hebrew
scribal culture centuries before European cryptography existed, and
it lived on in Jewish mysticism alongside Gematria, where letters are
numbers to be rearranged.

## The mirror idea travels

Once generalised, Atbash is simply "reverse the collating order" —
it works on Latin, Greek, Cyrillic, any ordered alphabet. Modern
cryptography meets it mostly as a teaching example and a puzzle-book
staple, and mathematically as the boundary case of the affine family.
A cipher from the prophets, still doing service as lesson one.

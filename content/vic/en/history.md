# History of the VIC cipher

## The hollow nickel

On 22 June 1953, Jimmy Bozart, a 14-year-old paperboy for the
*Brooklyn Eagle*, was collecting money on his round in Brooklyn. One
nickel felt too light. He dropped it on the floor, and it split in
two. Inside was a tiny piece of microfilm covered in numbers: ten
columns of five-digit groups.

The coin reached the New York police and, two days later, the FBI.
The Bureau photographed the film and tried for four years to read it.
The NSA tried too. Nobody could even tell what kind of cipher it was.
This is the Hollow Nickel Case, and the cipher on the film is the one
we now call VIC.

## Who was Reino Häyhänen

Reino Häyhänen was a KGB officer, born in 1920 near Leningrad to an
Ingrian Finnish family. In 1952 he arrived in New York under a false
name, Eugene Maki. His code name was "VICTOR", and the cipher is named
after it.

Häyhänen was an "illegal": a spy with no diplomatic cover. If the FBI
caught him, he went to prison, not home. His job was to help run a
Soviet spy network in the United States. By most accounts he did it
badly. He drank, he quarrelled with his wife, and he spent the
network's money on himself.

The nickel message was addressed to him. Nobody knows for sure how it
ended up in a paperboy's change. The likeliest answer is that a
hollow coin was spent by mistake.

## How the FBI finally read it

In 1957 Moscow ordered Häyhänen home. He suspected what that meant.
On his way, in early May, he walked into the US embassy in Paris and
asked for asylum. A few days later he was in New York, talking to the
FBI.

He explained the cipher and gave them his keys. By June the FBI had
read the nickel message. After four years of effort, it turned out to
be a welcome note from Moscow: congratulations on arriving safely,
followed by practical instructions for his first months.

Häyhänen's four keys show how the system was built to be memorised:

- the Russian word *снегопад* ("snowfall"), whose first seven letters
  set up the checkerboard;
- the opening of a popular song, «Одинокая гармонь» ("The Lonely
  Accordion");
- the date 3 September 1945, the day the Soviet Union celebrated
  victory over Japan;
- his personal number, 13.

None of these had to be written down. That was the point.

## Rudolf Abel and the bridge

Häyhänen also told the FBI about his boss, a man he knew only as
"Mark". The FBI traced Mark to a photo studio at 252 Fulton Street in
Brooklyn. There he lived as Emil Goldfus, a quiet artist and
photographer.

On 21 June 1957 agents arrested him at the Hotel Latham in Manhattan.
He gave his name as Rudolf Abel. His real name was William Fisher, a
KGB colonel. Häyhänen testified against him, and Abel was sentenced to
30 years in prison.

He served less than five. On 10 February 1962, the United States
traded Abel for Francis Gary Powers, the U-2 pilot shot down over the
Soviet Union. The swap took place on the Glienicke Bridge in Berlin.
Häyhänen did not see it: he died in a car crash in 1961.

## Why the VIC cipher matters

In 1961 the journalist David Kahn described the system in full in
"Number One From Moscow", an article in the CIA journal *Studies in
Intelligence*. He called it "the finest and most advanced mnemonic
cipher ever made public". It was not unbreakable in theory, he wrote,
but with a single message and no knowledge of the method, it could
not be broken in practice.

VIC grew out of older Soviet hand ciphers that mixed a checkerboard
with number additions. It took that idea as far as pencil and paper
can go. Soon after, machines and computers took over. The VIC cipher
is the high point of an era that was about to end.

The lesson of the Hollow Nickel Case is an old one. The mathematics
held for four years. What failed was a person.

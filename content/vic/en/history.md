# History of the VIC cipher

## The hollow nickel

On a Brooklyn street corner in 1953, a newspaper boy was paid with a
nickel that split in his hand. Inside was a scrap of microfilm:
twenty-one rows of ten columns, each column a five-figure digit
group. The boy's daughter had kicked it along the sidewalk a year
earlier; the FBI now had it, photographed, catalogued — and silent.
The message sat unread for four years, through every attempt the
Bureau and NSA could mount against a cipher they could not even name.

## Häyhänen and Abel

The system's designer is unknown — Kahn credits Soviet cipher
architects building on two decades of illegals' hand ciphers. Its
carrier is not. Reino Häyhänen, cover name "VICTOR", was a Finnish-born
Soviet illegal stationed in New York, running agents under diplomatic
immunity's absence. The nickel message was addressed to him — it had
been passed, mishandled, lost. His handler, operating as the
photographer Emil Goldfus, was Colonel Rudolf Abel, the KGB's most
senior illegal in North America.

In 1957 Häyhänen was recalled to Moscow, read the recall's meaning
correctly, and defected in Paris. In exchange for a new life he
reconstructed the VIC system for the FBI from memory — the hollow
nickel message finally opened, four years after its fifteen cents of
fame — and identified Abel, who was arrested in the Hotel Latham and
exchanged, in 1962, for the downed U-2 pilot Francis Gary Powers.

## The last hand cipher

VIC was the end of a lineage, not a beginning. Its ancestors run
through the Soviet networks of the 1940s — Richard Sorge's Tokyo ring
and the Rote Kapelle in Berlin carried checkerboard variants and
book-based additives, and several of those fell precisely because
their keys repeated or their systems were recovered by capture. VIC
was the synthesis of every lesson: mnemonic keys an agent could
remember under pressure, no paraphernalia to seize, no repetition to
find, and a fresh seed in every message.

After VIC, machine ciphers took the field — and the spy's pencil
never again carried a system this strong. Kahn's 1959 reconstruction
in *Studies in Intelligence* ("Number One From Moscow") made it the
best-documented hand cipher in the open literature, and the reason
this page can walk you through the whole schedule, worksheet line by
worksheet line, sixty years after its only betrayal.

# History of the Caesar cipher

## A private letter habit

The shift cipher is named after Julius Caesar, who — his biographer
Suetonius reports — used a shift of 3 in private correspondence: A
became D, B became E. His successor Augustus preferred a mild shift of
1, writing simply the next letter of the alphabet. For the Roman world
this was adequate protection: many of the recipients' enemies could not
read Latin at all, and literate Romans did not treat letter
substitution as serious cryptography anyway.

## Written down as an anecdote

Suetonius describes both schemes plainly in *The Lives of the Twelve
Caesars*, which is how we still know the details today. The cipher was
never a state secret — it circulated as a curiosity, a neat trick of
the great commander, and medieval manuscripts copied rotation tables
for "Caesar's alphabet" long after the empire was gone.

## Al-Kindi ends the single alphabet

Around the 9th century, in Baghdad, Al-Kindi wrote a treatise on
decrypting messages: count the letters, compare the counts with the
known letter frequencies of the language, and the substitution reveals
itself. That single idea broke every monoalphabetic cipher at once —
Caesar's included — and pushed cryptographers towards ciphers that hide
letter frequencies.

## The lesson that stayed

The Caesar cipher survives as the textbook first cipher. Twenty-five
keys are useless for real secrets, but the scheme is perfect for
learning what a key is, what brute force costs, and why frequency
analysis changed the field. The Renaissance answer — several alphabets
in turn — is the Vigenère cipher, and this site covers it in the same
way.

# History of the Saknussemm cipher

## A cipher invented for a novel

The Saknussemm cipher exists because Jules Verne needed a plot device:
a message no one alive can read. In "Journey to the Centre of the
Earth" (1864), Professor Lidenbrock buys a runic parchment inscribed
by the 16th-century Icelandic alchemist Arne Saknussemm and spends
days failing to read it — until his nephew Axel notices the runes
should be read backwards, and the Latin message "descend into the
crater of Yoculis at Sneffels" sends them both to Iceland.

## What the novel actually shows

Verne describes the parchment's text in cryptic clusters, three per
line — the famous dictation "mm.rnlls esruel seecJde sgtssmf…".
Working backwards from the printed page, the mechanics resolve into a
reversal plus a route transposition: the reversed Latin message is
written across a grid of 21 columns and read down, seven letters per
cluster, three clusters per line. Every letter of the plaintext is
accounted for — the cipher is pure shuffling, no substitution at all.
The runes are only costume: a cosmetic layer over ordinary letters,
exactly like pigpen's grid fragments.

## From plot device to puzzle

The parchment became one of literature's best-known ciphers, and it
has been picked up by puzzle writers ever since — as homage,
challenge and easter egg. This site keeps it off the graded cipher
ladder deliberately: it is not a rung to climb but a hidden page to
find, a fictional cipher whose whole key space is two small numbers
and one good idea ("read it backwards").

## The transcription this site reproduces

The scrambled dictation quoted in the tool comes from the novel's
first English translation (1871), the edition digitised by Project
Gutenberg. That text carries the parchment's punctuation as grid
cells and skips one letter — a corroded rune in the story — so
decoding it recovers the Latin message up to those documented quirks.
The engine itself is clean: any message, encrypted with the 7-by-3
shape, round-trips exactly.

# History of the Keyword substitution cipher

## Keyed alphabets enter the manuals

Once Renaissance cryptographers agreed that a plain shifted alphabet
was too predictable, the next idea was natural: scramble the alphabet
systematically, starting from a word both sides could remember. Keyed
alphabets — keyword first, remainder after — appear in the sixteenth-
century cipher manuals, among them Giambattista della Porta's *De
Furtivis Literarum Notis* (1563), and stayed a standard ingredient of
pen-and-paper cryptography for centuries.

## Frequency analysis had already ruined it

The attack that dooms every fixed substitution was old news by then.
Al-Kindi, a ninth-century Baghdad polymath, described breaking
monoalphabetic ciphers by letter frequencies in a treatise on
deciphering encrypted messages — keyword alphabets included, though
Europe would not read him for centuries. Every keyed alphabet since
lives under the same verdict: the mapping is fixed, so the counts
betray it.

## The Babington plot, 1586

The most famous English lesson in substitution weakness is the
correspondence that doomed Mary, Queen of Scots. Her letters with
Anthony Babington used a fixed cipher alphabet (a nomenclator with a
substitution alphabet at its core); Walsingham's cryptanalyst Thomas
Phelippes read them by frequency analysis, and a forged postscript
asking for the conspirators' names drew the final signatures. Mary was
executed on the evidence. The plot's lesson — a fixed alphabet, however
cleverly keyed, cannot survive a patient statistician — pushed
Elizabethan cryptography toward codebooks with thousands of entries.

## The keyword survives as a teacher

Keyword alphabets never vanished: they moved to puzzle books, war-time
field training, and classrooms. As a *key*, they fail; as a *memory
aid*, they are unmatched — a child can reconstruct the whole alphabet
from one word, which makes the keyword cipher the natural first
substitution with a real key to keep secret.

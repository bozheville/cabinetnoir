# History of the Beaufort cipher

## The table was old, the name is young

The cipher's arithmetic — subtracting the plaintext from the key instead
of adding — appears in Giovanni Sestri's 1710 treatise, almost a hundred
and fifty years before the name everyone uses. Like so many ciphers, it
was reinvented independently and the most famous name won.

## Admiral Beaufort and his card

Sir Francis Beaufort (1774–1857) was a Royal Navy hydrographer, the
officer who gave the world the Beaufort scale of wind force — the
"force 8 gale" scale still used in shipping forecasts. The cipher
published under his name (posthumously, in 1857, by his brother) was
something he apparently used privately for family correspondence. There
is no evidence he claimed originality; the cipher itself was likely
picked up from published sources of his time.

## The variant's other parent

The variant Beaufort (cipher = plaintext − key) has an even more
distinguished career: it is essentially the arithmetic the German army's
M-209 converter of the 1930s–1940s used inside its more elaborate
mechanical construction. A discarded variant of one century became a
component of a field machine in the next.

## The mirror family in practice

For a working cryptographer of the 1800s the three siblings — Vigenère,
Beaufort, variant Beaufort — were one tool with three settings. Manual
encyclopedias listed them together, and the choice between them was a
matter of taste: only classic Beaufort's self-reciprocity (one table
lookup serves both encryption and decryption in the field) was a
practical argument. Against a determined cryptanalyst all three fell
together, once Kasiski published the periodicity attack in 1863.

# History of the Pigpen cipher

## Older than its name

The grid-and-dot idea is old. Medieval sources describe ciphers built
from the parts of a cross, and in 1531 Cornelius Agrippa described an
early form of what later authors called the Rosicrucian cipher — a
single grid of nine "pens" whose dot positions carry the alphabet. He
attributed it to Jewish Kabbalistic tradition, the "Kabbalah of the
Nine Chambers", where it served religious symbolism more than secrecy.

## The Freemasons made it famous

The cipher's fame comes from Freemasonry. Lodges used pigpen from the
early 18th century to keep records of history and rites private and for
correspondence between lodge leaders — so persistently that the system
is now simply called the Freemason's cipher. It is carved where you
would expect it: Freemason tombstones carry pigpen engravings, and one
of the earliest stones in New York's Trinity Church cemetery deciphers
to "Remember death" — a masonic *memento mori*.

## Pirates and prisoners

On 7 July 1730 the French pirate Olivier Levasseur reportedly threw a
scrap of pigpen ciphertext into the crowd at his execution — allegedly
the whereabouts of his never-found treasure. The message has never been
decisively read, and the exact grid arrangement died with him: an early
lesson that a non-standard variant forces real cryptanalysis. During
the American Civil War, Union prisoners in Confederate prisons used
pigpen to smuggle messages past their captors, and George Washington's
army documentation shows a randomized-alphabet version of the system.

## The Templar legend — debunked

Websites happily attribute the cross-armed variant to the medieval
Knights Templar. The scholarly source for the "Templar cipher" itself
says otherwise: the alphabet-splitting, cross-armed system is the work
of Neo-Templar Freemasons of the 18th century, not the religious order
of the 12th–14th centuries. The medieval order left no such cipher; the
legend is modern branding on an old idea.

## Pigpen today

The cipher survived by becoming a game. It appears in children's books
on secret writing, puzzle hunts, escape rooms, treasure-fiction from
Verne onward, and even in a settler's 1852 Australian diary examined by
historians two centuries later. Pigpen is not used to protect anything
— it is used to *signal* secrecy, which may be the most durable job a
cipher can have.

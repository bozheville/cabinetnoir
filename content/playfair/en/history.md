# History of the Playfair cipher

## A physicist's parting gift

Charles Wheatstone — scientist, showman, bridge-builder (the
measurement bridge carries his name) — invented the cipher in 1854.
But it is named for his friend Lord Playfair, the scientist-politician
who promoted it to the British establishment. Playfair demonstrated
it at a dinner party to the leadership of the Foreign Office and the
Post Office; the reaction was famously dismissive — too difficult,
officials decided, for their clerks to master.

## The army disagreed

The military, less precious about its clerks, adopted what the
diplomats refused. The cipher served British forces in the Second
Boer War of 1899 as a field cipher for tactical messages, and again
through the First World War. Its virtues were practical: no tables
to carry beyond a memorised square, no equipment, quick enough for
a junior officer with a pencil.

## The first digraph cipher

What made Playfair new was not the square — squares of letters were
known — but the pairing: encrypting two letters at once. That single
change broke the letter-frequency shortcut that had ruled since
Al-Kindi, and it took cryptanalysis a new statistical tool (digraph
frequencies) to recover the advantage. For a classical cipher, that
is a long life: it protected field traffic for some sixty years.

## The descendants

The idea of encrypting grouped units became a design pattern:
two-square and four-square ciphers split and doubled the square for
more mixing, and Bifid-style ciphers layered fractionation on top.
The direct line ends at the machines — rotor systems substituted
whole alphabets on the fly — but the insight that *context changes
the meaning of a letter* is Playfair's permanent gift.

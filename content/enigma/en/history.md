# History of the Enigma machine

## A businessman's machine

Arthur Scherbius, a German engineer, patented his "Chiffriermaschine"
in 1918 and marketed the Enigma commercially from 1923 — to banks and
businesses who, mostly, did not buy. The German military did: the
Navy adopted the machine in 1926, the Army in 1928, and the Air Force
followed. Through the 1930s the Wehrmacht's confidence in it grew
into doctrine: the machine was considered unbreakable.

## The Poles break it first

In December 1932, mathematician **Marian Rejewski** of the Polish
Cipher Bureau — aided by French intelligence documents from a German
spy, Hans-Thilo Schmidt — reconstructed the Enigma's internal wiring
using pure mathematics on the machine's key procedure. With Henryk
Zygalski and Jerzy Różycki he built tools to recover daily keys: the
**cyclometer** and the perforated sheets ("Zygalski sheets"), then a
search machine they called the **bomba**. By 1938 Polish cryptanalysts
could read Enigma traffic routinely — years before anyone in
London or Paris believed it possible.

## Pyry: the handover that saved the method

German changes in 1938 (more rotors, altered key procedure) strained
the Polish tools. In July 1939, weeks before the war, the Poles
handed replicas, documentation and their methods to British and
French intelligence at the Pyry conference near Warsaw. The British
had been sceptical that a machine could be attacked mathematically;
the Polish results changed the entire course of the work that
followed.

## Bletchley Park and the bombe

At Bletchley Park, Alan Turing designed a new machine — the
**bombe** — around Turing-Welchley crib-based logic, refined from the
Polish idea. The first bombe, *Victory*, ran in March 1940; dozens
followed, working around the clock against the daily traffic. Naval
Enigma was the hardest prize: when the U-boats moved to the
four-rotor M4 in February 1942, the Atlantic convoy lifeline went
dark for about ten months until the capture of cipher materials and
new methods restored the reading.

## The scale and the silence

At its height Bletchley read thousands of messages a month and
supplied commanders with decrypted enemy intentions — intelligence
codenamed **Ultra**. Historian Harry Hinsley judged that it shortened
the war by two to four years. And then came silence: the entire
enterprise stayed secret into the 1970s. The veterans who returned to
civilian life could not tell anyone what they had done; the history
of the century's most consequential codebreaking was written decades
late.

A substitution cipher replaces every letter, or every pair of letters, with something else: another letter, a number, a symbol. The letters keep their place in the message; only their identity changes. The key is the replacement table.

With a Caesar shift of 3, HELLO becomes KHOOR: every letter moves three places down the alphabet. A keyword alphabet, a Polybius grid or a rotating disk are richer ways to build the same kind of table.

One fixed table leaks the letter frequencies of the language: E, T and A shine through, and frequency analysis reads them off. Polyalphabetic ciphers spread those frequencies over several tables, until Kasiski's test and the index of coincidence pull them apart again.

From Julius Caesar's dispatches to the Enigma machine, substitution was the backbone of secret writing for two thousand years.

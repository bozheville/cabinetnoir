# History of the Rail fence cipher

## The scytale: the fence's ancient ancestor

The oldest transposition on record is Sparta's scytale: a strip of
leather wound helically around a staff of the right thickness, written
across, and read as nonsense once unwound. Wind it back on a staff of
the same diameter and the message returns. It is not the rail fence —
the wrap is a spiral, not a zigzag — but the principle is identical:
the *shape of the carrier* is the key, and the letters never change.
Around 400 BC the Greek writers already knew both the cipher and its
downfall: the staff's diameter is a small, guessable secret.

## The American Civil War: rails in the field

By the nineteenth century the zigzag had its name and its folklore.
Rail fence — so called because the ciphertext looks like rails of a
fence laid side by side — was pencil-and-paper soldier's work on both
sides of the American Civil War: quick to write, quick to read, and
quick to break by anyone who guessed the fence's shape. Union route
ciphers, more elaborate cousins of the zigzag, carried Federal messages
in Confederate territory; their safety lay more in speed and disguise
than in mathematics.

## The schoolbook afterlife

Once serious cryptography moved to keyed alphabets and then machines,
the rail fence settled into a long afterlife in puzzle books, scout
manuals and classrooms. It keeps exactly one lesson worth teaching: a
transposition changes only *where* the letters are, so the letters
themselves stay in plain view — and a small enough key space is no key
at all. Every modern course still opens transposition with the zigzag,
for the same reason this app does.

# History of Morse code

## An artist with a magnet

Samuel Morse was a painter, not an engineer; the electromagnet that
started it all belonged to someone else. But after hearing a lecture
on the new electromagnetism in 1832, Morse spent years turning the
idea into a telegraph — and, with his assistant Alfred Vail, into a
code. The first version assigned numbers to words and looked them up
in a dictionary; Vail's second version, built on letter frequencies,
is the ancestor of what we use.

## "What hath God wrought"

On 24 May 1844 Morse tapped the famous question over the wire
between Washington and Baltimore. Within a decade telegraph lines
were stitching countries together, and the code went with them.

## Everyone's code becomes one code

National variants quickly sprouted — the original "American Morse"
on landlines, Gerke's cleaner revision in Germany and Austria. When
the world's telegraph administrations met in Paris in 1865, the
Gerke lineage was adopted as International Morse, the code still in
use (with tiny amendments) today.

## SOS and the wireless age

Wireless telegraphy inherited the code, and in 1906 the Berlin
convention made `... --- ...` the global distress signal — chosen
not as an abbreviation but as an unmistakable, easy-to-send rhythm.
Six years later the Titanic's operators transmitted it (alongside
the older CQD call) as the ship went down, and the world understood
what the dots were saying.

## A long fade-out

Commercial use declined through the twentieth century, but the sea
kept Morse longest: navies and ships' radio officers tapped away
until 1999, when the Global Maritime Distress and Safety System made
satellite equipment the standard and ended Morse's official watch at
sea. Amateur radio operators, aviation navaids and flashlights in
prisoner-of-war camps keep it alive anyway.

# Step-by-step guides

## Encode your first message

1. Type your message into {{control:message-input}}. Letters become
   dot-dash patterns separated by spaces; a space between words
   becomes a slash.
2. If you prefer a different look, choose the characters that play
   dot {{control:dot-symbol}} and dash {{control:dash-symbol}} — the
   patterns are unchanged.
3. Read the encoded line in {{control:output}}. Note that unknown
   characters (punctuation, accents) are dropped — the wire had no
   use for them either.

## Decode a message you received

1. Paste the Morse into {{control:message-input}} and press the swap
   button {{control:swap}}.
2. Keep the format the sender used: one space between letters, a
   slash between words. Any of the common dot and dash characters are
   accepted and normalised.
3. Read the decoded text in {{control:output}}. If a letter comes out
   wrong, look for a missing or doubled space — spacing is where
   hand-keyed Morse goes wrong.

## Read the rhythm by ear (bonus)

1. Compare the patterns from the worked example: E is one unit, T is
   three. The shortest patterns belong to the commonest letters.
2. When listening to real Morse, it is the *gaps* that define
   letters — an unbroken run of signal means nothing by itself.
3. This is why operators speak of "dits" and "dahs" with rhythm in
   their voice: the code was designed for the ear, not the eye.

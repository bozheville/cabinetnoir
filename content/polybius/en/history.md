# History of the Polybius square

## Torches over the Aegean

Polybius, the Greek historian of the second century BC, describes a
signalling scheme for sending any message across the sea and hills:
two groups of five torches, raised left and right, spell one letter at
a time — a number pair for each letter. The square was not built for
secrecy but for **speed and range**: a code both endpoints could work
out in the dark, letter by letter, with nothing written down.

## The idea that kept coming back

The scheme sleeps for two thousand years and then reappears everywhere.
Clergy and prisoners tap it out on walls — five-by-five, two knocks for
the row, two for the column, and a pause between letters. Russian
revolutionaries of the 1880s gave the square a numeric overlay and a
cipher of their own (the Nihilist cipher, later in the ladder). The
French army in 1918 built its most sophisticated field cipher, ADFGVX,
on the same 25 (later 36) cells, then scrambled the coordinates with a
transposition.

## Why a two-thousand-year-old table matters

The square is the bridge between two halves of classical
cryptography: it is the simplest substitution that produces a
*numeric* ciphertext, and numbers invite arithmetic — keys added to
coordinates, coordinates fractionated, mixed and transposed. Every
later stop on this ladder that touches a grid — {{term:polybius-square}}
at its centre — starts here.
